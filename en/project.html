Problem

During major accidents inside highway tunnels, both tunnel tubes must be closed. Injured people are brought to the safe tunnel tube through cross-passages – connecting corridors between the tubes. In most cases, firefighters transport the patients through the cross-passage, while the ambulance team waits at the other end.

The core issue is the lack of situational awareness. A typical tunnel has around 15 cross-passages, spaced roughly 300 meters apart. Emergency crews cannot see through tunnel walls, so they often don't know whether they are positioned at the correct passage. If, for example, the ambulance team waits at the wrong one, they may not realize their mistake – because they have no way of knowing where the fire brigade is located, and vice versa.

In the stressful environment of a tunnel operation – noise, tight space, poor visibility – this confusion can cost several minutes. Minutes that can be critical for severely injured patients.

It quickly became clear that a solution was needed – one that gives all responders a clear operational picture, including the situation in the second tube and areas hidden by curves. The idea emerged to visualize emergency vehicles on a web interface. Responders rarely stray far from their vehicles, so the vehicle positions would serve as a reliable indicator.

A GPS-based solution was ruled out because GPS signals do not penetrate tunnels, and equipping every emergency vehicle with additional hardware would be expensive. Acoustic recognition was also unsuitable, as sirens are not activated continuously.

Solution

The most obvious resource was already available: the tunnel’s surveillance cameras. They are positioned to cover the entire tunnel with no blind spots. This made it natural to use the camera feeds for vehicle detection – powered by artificial intelligence.

Because no dataset of Austrian emergency vehicles existed, one had to be created first. Through my work in emergency medical services, I had direct contact with ambulance, fire brigade, and police units. Together with ASFiNAG, we organized a meeting at the tunnel control center. There, every emergency vehicle – including the large fire engine – was able to turn around under the surveillance cameras. This provided image material from every possible angle, ensuring reliable recognition even when vehicles are positioned diagonally or sideways.

From the recorded video material, I extracted about 100,000 individual frames. After removing unsuitable images, approximately 80,000 remained. I manually annotated every single one – marking whether it showed a fire engine, ambulance, or police vehicle. Based on this dataset, I trained an AI model that reached an accuracy of over 90 percent.

To precisely determine the vehicle positions, the distance to each camera was calculated. These position data are visualized on a custom-built web interface. It displays a to-scale tunnel layout where all detected emergency vehicles appear in real time. As a prototype, we used the small Trettnig tunnel in Carinthia, which has only one cross-passage. To recreate the tunnel layout with absolute accuracy, we walked through the entire tunnel, measured every camera position and the exact location of the cross-passage, and transferred all data into the model. This was possible because a tunnel cleaning operation was underway at the time.

Result

The result is a platform-independent web application that works on smartphones, laptops, and tablets. It provides emergency responders with a precise, real-time overview of the situation inside the tunnel—enabling faster, safer, and more efficient operations. The ASFiNAG tunnel control center also benefits from a significantly improved situational picture.
